# Does a Two-Part MDL Cost Structure Describe Manufacturing Cost Allocation? An Evidence-Graded Test

## TL;DR
- **The public data is abundant and strongly consistent with a two-part "standing + occasioned" cost structure, but in its present form it can only weakly distinguish the specific MDL form from a generic fixed-plus-marginal model — and on the single prediction that could discriminate most sharply (a non-zero residual floor), the best meta-analyses simply never tested for it.** The correspondence claim is therefore *supported at the level of qualitative structure* but *remains largely untested at the level that would select MDL over any amortisation story*.
- **Prediction 1 (graded, ordered build-out) is well supported qualitatively and semi-quantitatively**: crossover volumes exist, are computable, and adoption is staged and ordered (prototype tooling → CNC → single-cavity mould → multi-cavity + robotics; FPGA → ASIC; manual → SMT), broadly tracking an N × per-unit-saving > mechanism-cost rule. But almost none of the public data orders these steps by *information density* (residual removed per unit of mechanism complexity), so it is *consistent with* MDL without *selecting for* it.
- **Prediction 2 (non-zero residual floor) is the crux and is only partially supported**: aircraft/airframe learning curves demonstrably "level off" due to tooling limits, and cost-of-quality data show appraisal+prevention never reaching zero — both consistent with an irreducible floor. But the large experience-curve meta-analyses (Nagy et al. 2013; Farmer & Lafond 2016; Lafond et al. 2018) fit only pure power laws to zero and *never tested a floor-augmented model*, so the strongest available datasets are silent on exactly the discriminating question.

## Key Findings
1. **Two-part decomposition is the industry-standard cost model.** Every quantified sourcing literature reviewed (injection moulding, semiconductors, PCB assembly, EOQ/batch, cost-of-quality) natively expresses total cost as a fixed/standing term plus a per-unit term. This is structurally identical to L(model) + N × residual. This is corroboration of the *form* but is exactly what any fixed-plus-marginal model also predicts.
2. **Crossover volumes are real, computed, and staged (supports Prediction 1).** Injection moulding vs 3D printing/CNC crossovers cluster at roughly 100–2,000 units depending on geometry; FPGA→ASIC breakevens run from ~1,400 units (simple, low NRE) to 50,000–100,000+ units (advanced nodes); manual→automated PCB assembly breaks even around 100–1,000 units. Adoption is ordered and incremental, not all-at-once.
3. **The residual floor exists in two literatures but is unmeasured in the best one (mixed evidence for Prediction 2).** Airframe learning curves "level off" after ~125 units due to fixed tooling (Asher 1956), and cost-of-quality practitioners hold that prevention+appraisal costs will "never [be] fully eliminate[d]" even at zero defects — both consistent with an MDL-mandated floor. But the canonical Wright's-law meta-analyses fit pure power laws that trend to zero and never test a floor term, so the strongest datasets cannot confirm or refute the floor.
4. **No public dataset orders mechanism adoption by information density.** The MDL-specific claim — that distinctions flip from human judgment to encoded automation in an order set by residual-removed-per-unit-of-mechanism-complexity — is not something any operations dataset was collected to test. This is the principal gap requiring purpose-collected data.

## Details

### Framing: what would distinguish MDL from generic fixed-plus-marginal?
Emil's hypothesis is that total production cost decomposes as **L(model)** — standing investment paid once (specification, tooling, automation, process engineering) — plus **N × per-act residual** — occasioned cost incurred per unit (inspection, operator judgment, rework, manual adjustment). This is the two-part code of Rissanen's MDL: L(x) = min over H of [L(H) − log P(x|H)]. The problem is that *any* amortisation story — textbook fixed-cost-plus-marginal-cost, EOQ, breakeven analysis — produces total cost = fixed + N × variable. That generic form is trivially confirmed by all the data and confirms nothing specific about MDL. The task therefore reduces to the two discriminating predictions.

### Prediction 1: Graded build-out / marginal condition
**Claim:** standing investment should not merely rise with volume; distinctions should *flip* from per-unit human judgment to encoded automation at *computable crossover volumes*, adopted in a *staged order* governed roughly by "invest when N × per-unit-saving > mechanism cost," with steps ordered by information density.

**What quantified evidence exists — and it is abundant.**

*Plastics (within-part volume variation — the cleanest confound control).* The same part is costed across volume tiers, holding task demand fixed. Reported crossover volumes:
- 3D printing cheaper below ~30 units; CNC machining best ~30–250; injection moulding cheapest thereafter, with ~$10,000 tooling amortised over 250+ units [Xometry](https://www.xometry.com/resources/injection-molding/injection-molding-vs-3d-printing/) (Xometry).
- Aluminium/"bridge" tooling ($3,000–$10,000) suits 1,000–10,000 parts; hardened steel ($20,000–$100,000+) for lifespans exceeding 1,000,000 cycles [Evokpoly](https://evokpoly.com/feeds/blog/injection-molding-vs-3d-printing) (multiple mould vendors).
- One vendor breakdown: a part costing $0.15 each at 100,000 units costs $150 each at 10 units, because tooling dominates at low volume; crossover where moulding beats CNC/print "typically 500–2,000 units for simple geometries and 2,000–5,000 units for parts with side actions or tight tolerances" (MakerStage).

This is a textbook *staged* crossover: as volume rises, the standing investment ratchets up in discrete steps (no tool → soft tool → hard tool → multi-cavity), each justified when cumulative per-unit saving exceeds the incremental mechanism cost. Crucially, moulding vendors also describe a *second* staged step *within* the high-volume regime: multi-cavity moulds "above 50,000 units" [MakerStage](https://www.makerstage.com/resources/injection-molding-costs) and robotic part-removal/cavity-separation cells added at high volume — automating the *material-handling and inspection distinctions* that were manual at lower volume. That is precisely the "distinction flips from human judgment to encoded mechanism" pattern MDL predicts.

*Semiconductors (NRE vs per-die).* FPGA→ASIC conversion is the canonical computed crossover: Break-Even Volume = NRE ÷ (FPGA unit price − ASIC unit price). Worked example from Tekmos: "Assume that the FPGA costs $40 each, and the ASIC costs $5 each… A typical FPGA to ASIC NRE will be on the order of $49,000. That puts the breakeven point at 1400 units." Advanced nodes push NRE to $2M–$15M+ and crossovers to 50,000–100,000+ units [Wevolver](https://www.wevolver.com/article/asic-vs-fpga) for edge-AI (Wevolver; IPValueLabs). One 2026 industry note put 3 nm NRE at $400–600M+ with a $10–20M mask set. This is exactly N × per-unit-saving > mechanism-cost, computed explicitly by practitioners.

*PCB assembly.* Manual→automated SMT breaks even around 100–500 units depending on board complexity and labour rates; automation "often cutting per-unit costs by 50% or more" above ~1,000 units (ALLPCB). Hybrid manual/automated lines (hand-place sensitive parts, automate the rest) show *partial, ordered* mechanisation rather than all-at-once switching.

*Operations-management theory.* The Hayes–Wheelwright product–process matrix (HBR 1979) formalises the ordered progression job shop → batch → assembly line → continuous flow as volume rises, "from highly manual processes with high unit costs to highly automated processes with low unit costs," [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayes-Wheelwright_matrix) with firms migrating down the diagonal as a product matures. Fixed vs flexible vs manual automation ROI curves cross at volume/variety thresholds. The automation-economics literature (Autor, Levy & Murnane 2003 and successors) independently establishes that the *first* tasks to be encoded are "routine, codifiable" ones — consistent with MDL's ordering by how compressible a distinction is.

**Verdict on Prediction 1:** *Strongly consistent with, but does not select for, MDL.* The graded, ordered, computable build-out is exactly what MDL predicts — and the moulding/PCB "flip from judgment to mechanism" cases are genuinely suggestive of information-density ordering. However, every one of these crossovers is *also* exactly what generic breakeven/fixed-plus-marginal analysis predicts. The MDL-*specific* claim — that adoption order tracks *residual-removed-per-unit-of-mechanism-complexity* (an information-density ranking) rather than simply per-dollar ROI — is *not tested* by any of this data, because none of it measures mechanism "complexity" in bits or the residual removed per bit. Grade: **Consistent (moderate); discriminating power low.**

### Prediction 2: Non-zero residual floor
**Claim:** per-unit occasioned cost (inspection, judgment, rework) should asymptote to a *non-zero* floor at high volume. A plain fixed-cost model permits driving per-unit judgment cost to zero; MDL forbids it under any finite model. Floor present → supports MDL; per-unit quality/judgment cost → 0 at scale → against.

**Evidence FOR a floor.**
- *Airframe learning curves level off.* Boeing's study of the L-15 found the unit-cost curve became flat ("level-off") at large cumulative output, attributed explicitly to "limitations imposed by a given set of tooling" [Carnegie Mellon University](https://www.cmu.edu/epp/iecm/rubin/PDF%20files/2010/Yeh&Rubin_Uncertainties%20in%20Experience%20Curves_18May2010.pdf) (reported in Yeh & Rubin 2010). Harold Asher's *Cost-Quantity Relationships in the Airframe Industry* (RAND Report R-291, 1956) analysed nine fighter models and found learning "began to level off after about 125 units"; extrapolating a log-linear fit from 100 to 1,000 units produced ~25% error. Carr (1946), verbatim: "without redesign or additional tooling, there is a definite limit below which operations cannot be performed at reduced man-hours." [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227440990_The_mythology_of_learning-by-doing_in_World_War_II_airframe_and_ship_production) This is direct evidence that, *for a fixed model/tooling*, per-unit cost bottoms out at a non-zero floor — exactly MDL's prediction that a finite model cannot drive the residual to zero. (A patent-embedded costing model even encodes two "knees": human learning saturates ~120–200 units, process optimisation continues to ~500–1,200 units.)
- *Cost of quality never reaches zero.* The Prevention–Appraisal–Failure literature holds that while failure cost can in principle → 0, per iSixSigma (verbatim) "you will never fully eliminate appraisal and prevention costs (as opposed to failure costs that in an ideal zero-defect world would also be zero)." Even Six Sigma organisations spend "less than 1%" of sales on non-conformance but not zero; ASQ benchmarks put cost of poor quality at 10–30% of annual revenue for typical manufacturers, with world-class operations reducing it to below 5% — a floor, not zero. Median scrap+rework runs ~1.5–2.5% of COGS (APQC) even at high volume. This maps onto MDL's *occasioned* residual: irreducible per-unit inspection/judgment/rework that persists at any finite process specification.
- *The Juran optimum vs Crosby "quality is free" debate is literally the floor question.* Juran's classic model has a total-quality-cost minimum at less-than-perfect conformance (a non-zero appraisal+failure floor); [Institute of Industrial and Systems Engineers](https://www.iise.org/details.aspx?id=22118) Crosby argued prevention investment can approach zero-defect. Empirical PAF studies (e.g. the 2025 Indonesia manufacturing study, 300 products) find a **U-shaped** total-cost curve for combined prevention+appraisal strategies (cost falls to a minimum at "stage 3," then rises) — i.e. an interior optimum with a non-zero floor, siding with Juran [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311975.2025.2543515) and consistent with MDL.

**Evidence that WEAKENS the floor test.**
- *The best datasets never tested a floor.* This is the decisive gap. The canonical experience-curve meta-analyses — Nagy, Farmer, Bui & Trancik (2013, PLoS ONE 8(2):e52669, 62 technologies, Santa Fe Performance Curve Database); Farmer & Lafond (2016, 53 technologies); [Maastricht University](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/how-predictable-is-technological-progress/) Lafond et al. (2018, *Technological Forecasting and Social Change* 128:104–117, 51 technologies) — fit only pure power-law (Wright: y = B·x^(−w)) [mit](http://web.mit.edu/mitssrc/nsf/papers/Nagy_Farmer_Bui_Trancik_2013.pdf) and exponential (Moore) forms, *all of which trend monotonically to zero*. None includes an additive floor constant (the "+c" in y = a·x^b + c). They explicitly justify this: Lafond et al. (2018), verbatim, "For short data sets such as most of those used here, fitting more than one parameter often results in degradation [in out-of-sample performance]," echoing Nagy et al. (2013). Nagy et al. found Wright's law gave the most accurate out-of-sample forecasts, "with the square root of the logarithmic error growing linearly with the forecasting horizon at a typical rate of 2.5% per year" — but among six *floor-free* forms only. So the largest, best-curated unit-cost dataset in existence is *structurally incapable* of confirming or refuting a floor: it assumed one away. The recent 155-technology extension (Gogerty, SSRN WP 6198738, 2025/2026) reports a median learning rate of ~20% (a global aggregate of ~13% cost fall per doubling) and GPU compute at 89.2% per doubling, but likewise fits floor-free forms.
- *Abernathy & Wayne (1974) "Limits of the Learning Curve" is mis-citable here.* Their "limit" is *organisational* (over-specialisation destroys the ability to innovate/retool — the Ford Model T case), not an empirical cost floor. [Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/719814204/Abernathy-and-Wayne-Limits-of-the-Learning-Curve) The Model T itself followed a constant ~15%-per-doubling power law [Iiasa](http://www.iiasa.ac.at/web/home/research/researchPrograms/TransitionstoNewTechnologies/LearningCurveModelT.en.html) with *no* observed asymptote over 1909–1923. So it is not evidence for a floor.
- *Floor/plateau models exist but mostly outside manufacturing-cost forecasting.* Plateau models (y = max(K·x^n, K0)) and Boone's decaying-exponent curve are used in DoD cost estimating (Hogan et al. 2020, AFIT/MDPI *Forecasting*, tested Boone's model on 169 DoD end-items and found statistically significant but high-variance error reduction versus the constant-slope Crawford/Wright model), confirming learning *flattens* at high cumulative volume. But these operationalise flattening via a decaying learning rate or a step function, not a *reported non-zero dollar floor value* — so even here the floor's magnitude is essentially unmeasured.

**Verdict on Prediction 2:** *Genuinely mixed.* The floor is well-attested where researchers looked for it directly (airframe tooling level-off; the persistence of appraisal/prevention/rework cost; the Juran U-curve), and these are the pieces most distinctive of MDL versus a fixed-cost model that permits per-unit judgment → 0. But the *strongest and largest* datasets (experience-curve meta-analyses) never tested a floor and cannot arbitrate. And a competing benign explanation — that per-unit cost bottoms out at *material cost* (pure marginal cost), not at an *information-theoretic judgment residual* — fits the same data. Distinguishing "floor = irreducible occasioned judgment/inspection (MDL)" from "floor = raw material + energy (ordinary economics)" requires decomposing the residual, which no public dataset does. Grade: **Partially supported; discriminating power moderate but currently unrealised.**

### The confound Emil flagged, and how the evidence handles it
Cross-firm/cross-product comparisons conflate task demand with allocation. The strongest evidence here *does* control this: the injection-moulding-vs-machining-vs-print comparisons cost the *same part* across volume tiers (within-task volume variation), and the airframe/L-15 learning curves track *one production line over time*. These are the cleanest tests and they support the two-part structure and (for airframes) the floor. The weakest evidence — the experience-curve meta-analyses and the automation-typology material — aggregates across products and cannot isolate allocation from demand.

### Is there any direct application of MDL/information theory to manufacturing cost?
Effectively none was found. MDL and two-part codes appear extensively in statistics, machine learning, and *process mining* (e.g. entropic-relevance conformance checking of process models), but no study was located that applies an information-theoretic L(model) + N × residual decomposition to *physical manufacturing cost allocation*. The correspondence Emil proposes appears to be genuinely novel and unbuilt — which is why the discriminating data does not yet exist.

## Recommendations
**Staged, concrete next steps, with the thresholds that would change the verdict.**

1. **First, mine the one dataset that already discriminates: within-part cost-vs-volume curves.** Obtain raw quote data (e.g. from a moulding/CNC/print service that costs the *same* CAD part across 10–100,000 units) and fit *three* nested models: (a) pure power law y = a·x^b; (b) fixed-plus-marginal y = F/x + m; (c) floor-augmented y = a·x^b + c. **Threshold:** if model (c)'s fitted floor c is statistically significant and *decomposes into a non-material judgment/inspection component*, that is the first real evidence *selecting for* MDL over generic amortisation. If c ≈ material cost alone, the MDL-specific claim is not supported.
2. **Second, test the information-density ordering directly (the untested core of Prediction 1).** Take one production line's automation history (order in which distinctions were encoded: part-removal robot, vision inspection, cavity separation, gauging). For each step, estimate residual removed per unit (the per-unit human cost eliminated) and mechanism "complexity" (a proxy for L(mechanism): engineering hours, lines of PLC/vision code, or degrees of freedom). **Threshold:** if adoption order correlates with *residual-removed-per-unit-of-complexity* better than with raw per-dollar ROI, that selects for MDL's information-density ordering. If the two rankings are indistinguishable (they often will be, since complexity and cost co-vary), the prediction remains *consistent-but-not-selecting*.
3. **Third, re-analyse cost-of-quality panel data for the floor.** Use a firm or ASQ/APQC panel that tracks prevention+appraisal+failure cost as process sigma improves over time. **Threshold:** a strictly positive asymptote in appraisal+prevention as failure → 0 (Juran U-curve confirmed) supports the floor; a monotone approach to zero (Crosby) refutes it.
4. **Do not rely on the experience-curve meta-analyses as-is.** They are the largest datasets but were fit floor-free; re-fitting the Santa Fe PCDB with a "+c" term would be a cheap, high-value study, but note the meta-analysts' warning that extra parameters degrade out-of-sample fit on short series — so this needs the longest-run technologies (solar PV, DRAM, airframes) where a floor could actually be identified.

**Bottom line for Emil:** the numbers are indeed "out there" for the *generic* two-part structure and for *crossover volumes*, but they are *not* out there in a form that isolates the two MDL-specific signatures (information-density ordering; irreducible *judgment* residual distinct from material cost). The correspondence is promising and structurally sound, but selecting MDL over a generic amortisation model requires purpose-collected, within-task, residual-decomposed data of the kind described above.

## Caveats
- **"Consistent with" ≠ "selects for" throughout.** Nearly all the abundant data confirms the *generic* fixed-plus-marginal form, which MDL shares with every amortisation model. Genuine discrimination requires the two specific signatures, and on both the public data is either silent (Prediction 1's information-density ordering) or under-tested (Prediction 2's floor).
- **Vendor/marketing sources dominate the crossover-volume data.** The injection-moulding, PCB, and FPGA crossover figures come largely from service-bureau and vendor blogs (Xometry, Fictiv, Tekmos, MakerStage, ALLPCB, etc.), which have an interest in the "switch to us at volume X" narrative. The numbers are internally consistent and physically plausible but are not peer-reviewed; treat specific crossover values as order-of-magnitude.
- **The floor's *composition* is the unresolved question.** Airframe level-off and residual quality cost establish a non-zero floor *exists*, but whether that floor is an information-theoretic judgment residual (MDL) or simply material+energy marginal cost (ordinary economics) is not resolved by any source found.
- **Publication/selection bias in learning curves.** Yeh & Rubin (2010) note studies showing *price increases* (negative learning) were excluded from classic surveys (e.g. Dutton & Thomas), and nuclear power exhibits sustained "anti-learning" — so the tidy power-law picture is itself partly an artefact of which technologies get studied.
- **No existing MDL-to-manufacturing application was found**, so there is no prior literature to lean on; the correspondence must be treated as a novel hypothesis awaiting purpose-collected data, not as a settled result.
